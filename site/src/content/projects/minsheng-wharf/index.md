Minsheng Wharf holds 740 metres of the Huangpu River's south bank in northern Pudong, four kilometres upstream of Lujiazui and facing the Yangpu industrial belt across the water. Established in the early 1900s to load bulk grain and sugar for the Port of Shanghai, it carries more than ten working warehouses from successive eras: the Victorian red-brick sheds raised by British traders, the 40,000- and 80,000-ton grain silos that were once the largest in Asia, and the bow-string truss warehouses and conveyor structures added in the 1990s. David Chipperfield Architects Shanghai was invited to compete for the wharf's redevelopment masterplan — a joint undertaking of Taikoo and Lujiazui Group that doubles the existing floor area with offices, retail, hospitality and a contemporary art museum while preserving the industrial fabric as fully as possible.

The proposal works by subtraction and addition rather than replacement, easing the inhuman scale of the plant and mending the broken urban fabric so that old and new alternate along a renewed frontage. Leftover ground between the warehouses becomes a public system of streets and plazas running from the avenue through to the water, and programme is matched to the spaces that suit it: the dense, opaque silos hold the industrial history museum and art depot, the small-span brick warehouses hold hotel and offices, and the long-span riverfront sheds take retail and recreation.

As one of three lead designers on the project, I was responsible for the riverfront warehouses, for the conversion of the wharf's cargo transport system into an industrial heritage visit route, and for the riverfront landscape, developed with the landscape consultant and central to the proposal's progress through the first and second competition rounds. In the warehouses the outer walls are left intact while the river-facing span is opened as outdoor space and the second span filled with new programme; gantry beams and other industrial structures stay in place and are absorbed into the public circulation. Along the water, a 1.5-metre flood barrier that had sealed the site off gives way to cascading recycled-concrete topography that returns the bank to a walkable, human scale. The logistics network of passageways, corridors and conveyor ramps is preserved and re-inhabited as a nine-stop visit route: it begins at the ferry barge, threads through exhibitions of the machinery it once carried, and delivers visitors — at height, and across the span of the plant — into the museum inside the silos.
